## 3. Top 3 recommended posts

### POST 1 — "How to Defuse a Twenty-Seven-Page Slop Bomb"

**Alt titles:** "I Got Handed a Slop Bomb in a Meeting" / "My Two-Page Rule for Every AI Report"

**Hook (written, ~100 words, scene-first):**

> Someone dropped a twenty-seven-page report into a Monday meeting, hot off the presses, and the whole room was reading it for the first time at the same time. It looked good — real charts, real structure, the kind of polish that makes people nod along by page three. That was the problem. "You can tell, like, wow, this really all sounds right," Turner said after, "but I can't tell if it's smart or totally unintelligible in some places." That sentence is the actual crisis of AI-generated work right now: fluency and substance now cost the same two seconds to produce, and a room full of professionals just got asked to tell them apart live.

**Full outline:**
1. **The scene** — the 27-page report, read cold, in real time, by a room that can't yet tell polish from substance. (Turner, 13:21.258)
2. **The stall it reveals** — stakes are "low" in the sense that everyone's experimenting, but a slop bomb still soaks up real time and effort before anyone catches it. (Turner, 13:52.681)
3. **The mechanism, as a 3–5 bullet breakdown:**
   - Tell the agent, explicitly, "max two pages" — not as a style preference but as a forcing function. (Turner, 13:52.681 — "Say, hey agent, max two pages, right? How hard is that?")
   - Treat yourself as the *filter*, not the forwarder: "I am not gonna ask anyone to read anything that I have not read word for word." (Turner, 14:21.992)
   - Recognize the underlying shift in your own job: the deck-guy analogy — don't grab the tools from the specialist, but don't abdicate the output either. (Turner, 10:52.438)
   - Reframe the job itself as a decision engine, the way pre-internet executives operated: "it was people in suits sitting there reading memo after memo after memo being decision engine for team after team after team." (Turner, 12:11.422)
4. **The outside evidence / receipt** — Jeremy's independent version of the same rule, arrived at separately: "I don't have time to read your twelve pages, buddy. Let me give me the fifty, fifty to a hundred words." (Jeremy, 12:45.611)
5. **The opinion stake, stated as a claim:** the executive job isn't disappearing into AI — it's reverting to what it was before the internet made everyone their own analyst. The actual new skill isn't prompting; it's knowing how many pages you're willing to accept before you stop being able to tell smart from unintelligible.

**Crafting direction:** This is the post most at risk of AI-polished cadence (checklist #7) because its own thesis is about AI-polished output — avoid "it's not X, it's Y" constructions and tidy triads while writing about exactly that failure mode; roughen toward how Turner actually said it. Clean up the ASR artifact before publication: the transcript renders "slop bomb" inconsistently as "slop a bomb" (12:53.908) and "slot bomb" (13:52.681, three times) — these are transcription errors, not the actual words spoken; normalize to "slop bomb" throughout the prose (the brief's clean-up-pass discipline explicitly allows this — ASR noise isn't "the hesitation that makes it feel lived-in," it's a transcription bug). Expand beyond the tape with one outside reference: the pre-internet-executive parallel (bullet #4 above) is Turner's own line and carries the essay — don't dilute it with an added historical citation, just let the claim stand starkly, per the brief's swyx lesson (write it as a claim other practitioners will want to cite or argue with by name). Target **1,900 words** — rich enough on tape (two merged topics, five distinct named moments) to earn the long end of the band without padding.

**Why this audience, this post:** the brief's framing research says this audience (practitioner/consultant/eng-leader) responds to concrete "how," not abstract "why" — this post opens on a literal scene (a specific meeting, a specific page count) rather than an "in the age of AI" frame, which is both the HN craft lesson and the single most-repeated reader-panel kill condition (`01_audience_response_brief.md` §4). It also gives the series a citable, arguable claim ("the real skill is deciding how many pages you'll accept") rather than a hedge — exactly what the sticky-idea mechanics in §2 of the brief call for.

### POST 2 — "I Burned a Five-Hour Rate Limit in One Hour"

**Alt titles:** "The Day 'Two More Things' Ate My Token Budget" / "My Agent's 'Clever' Shortcut Cost Me 700,000 Tokens"

**Hook (written, ~100 words, scene-first):**

> Jeremy's architect agent made a decision on its own: instead of spawning a fresh subagent for a new piece of work, it would reuse the existing one, so it could "reuse the context that it already had." On paper that's good engineering — don't reload what's already cached. In practice, it meant reloading roughly 700,000 tokens of context, repeatedly, across two projects running in parallel, and burning through a five-hour rate-limit window in about an hour. Nobody told it to do that. It decided efficiency meant something different than efficient, and there was no rule on file that said otherwise.

**Full outline:**
1. **The scene** — Jeremy's agent, on Opus, silently appends a rule into its own spec file: "don't use new subagents, reuse the existing subagent." (Jeremy, 40:26.882)
2. **The stall it reveals** — this looks clever (the context *should* already be cached) but isn't, because of exactly how caching resets: "if you do it like literally the next day after the cache is reset, maybe now you're sort of exposed." (Turner, 40:52.839 — flag: transcript renders "cached/cache" as "cashed/cash," an ASR artifact to fix before publication)
3. **The receipt** — the actual cost, in Jeremy's own numbers: "I was running through I was building two projects at the same time and I was just running through my five hour window in like an hour." (Jeremy, 41:15.095) The pattern repeats: four hours of forced wait, then "restart work immediately after that... loading that heavy payload again later." (Jeremy, 41:39.567)
4. **The named precedent, as outside evidence:** Gastown — the software factory that was shut down because it only worked inside its own narrow ecosystem (Jeremy, 38:35.620) — and the specific Opus quirk that compounded it: a compulsive "and two more things" appended to the end of every task, unanticipated by any pipeline built around it. (Jeremy, 39:5.333) Turner's reaction names the real problem precisely: "that's a loose canon... play ad libs with more work to charge us tokens for." (Turner, 39:20.751)
5. **The fix that's actually being reached for** — this is where the episode pivots, unprompted, into role-scoped system prompts: "rather than just starting out with a generic Claude dangerously skip permissions, now I can say, hey, start out with this architect archetype or persona." (Jeremy, 39:41.649–39:59.265)
6. **The opinion stake, stated as a claim:** autonomous "efficiency" decisions made by a model, with no rule on file to check them, are not efficient — they're just unaudited. The fix isn't less autonomy; it's a rule that makes the model's own notion of "clever" visible before it runs, not after the token bill arrives.

**Crafting direction:** This is the leanest, most technical post of the three — lean into that rather than padding it toward 2,200 words; **target 1,700 words.** The audience here skews practitioner/eng-leader, so don't over-explain "subagent" or "context window" at length — one tight clause of definition per term, then move. This is the one post where the receipts requirement (checklist #4) is already solved by the tape itself — three hard numbers (700k tokens, a 5-hour window, two parallel subscriptions) and one named precedent (Gastown) all land before the halfway mark without any outside research needed. The craft risk is the opposite of Post 1: don't let the hook undersell how funny and specific "and two more things" is — that line should survive as dialogue, not get paraphrased into "an unexpected behavior."

**Why this audience, this post:** this is the single densest "bottom-up practitioner mechanics" moment on the tape — exactly the wedge this newsletter is built on (`round10_review.html` §0: "take a thing the thinkpieces predict, show the version you actually ran, state your opinion of where it breaks"). It also matches the brief's reverse-engineered HN pattern precisely: a short title that does the thesis's work in under ten words, an in-medias-res open, and a hands-on/falsifiable detail (an exact token count, an exact time window) that converts passive readers into commenters, because every practitioner reading this has their own version of a rate-limit horror story to argue with. Notably — this topic is not in Wispr Flow's independent list of 10, and Jeremy's own top 3 didn't surface it either. It's new signal from the raw tape, not a confirmation of either prior read. See §4.

### POST 3 — "My 150 Apple Notes Became a Compost Heap"

**Alt titles:** "I Built a Riff Engine for My Own Novel" / "AI Didn't Ghostwrite My Book — It Drew the Blueprint"

**Hook (written, ~100 words, scene-first):**

> Turner has a hundred and fifty Apple Notes tagged #CH — the working code name for his novel, *Counterfeit Heart* — collected over years, each one a scene idea, a character beat, or a note on why something didn't work in the first draft. "Treat the Apple Notes as like a compost heap for all my stories," he says. Before AI, turning that compost heap into a structured draft meant sitting at a laptop for hours, reading every note in order, and manually sorting. This time, he fed all 150 notes and his old first draft into Claude Code and asked it to do the first pass of sorting with him — not for him.

**Full outline:**
1. **The scene** — 150 Apple Notes, tagged #CH, each a fragment ("an idea for a scene, an idea for a character, like something about the plot that... didn't work in the first book"), described explicitly as a "compost heap." (Turner, 23:49.065; 24:17.716)
2. **The stall it reveals** — the old process worked, but it was slow and manual: "I throw spaghetti against the wall and this seems to be a nice trellis." (Turner, 25:16.060) The real innovation isn't AI writing — it's AI doing the *sorting and riffing* pass across years of fragments that a human would otherwise have to re-read start to finish.
3. **The mechanism, as a 3–5 bullet breakdown:**
   - Build a custom tool, not a chat window: "let's make a riff engine... bring up one of the Apple notes on the left and then just give me a box on the right to riff on it." (Turner, 26:4.765)
   - Make the tool follow the work, not the other way around — same environment, ported to Tesla, iPad, and iPhone, so the willpower to work and the ability to work are never blocked by being at the wrong desk. (Turner, 26:31.793–26:42)
   - Target a named structural framework, not a vibe: Larry Brooks's "nine sentences" story-architecture method, which Turner has used for a decade, now with the riff engine feeding directly into it. (Turner, 24:47.056)
   - Run the finished nine-sentence draft through simulated beta readers and critics before paying a human developmental editor $2,000–3,000 for the same workshop-style feedback. (Turner, 27:55.070; 28:48.241)
4. **The outside evidence / receipt** — Jeremy's independent, convergent practice: simulating ICP conversations to sharpen sales offers before a real client sees them, and his read on why the diversified-angle version of feedback beats a single human voice "at a fraction of the cost." (Jeremy, 29:54.679–30:24.566)
5. **The honest tension, which earns the stake its edge** — is tuning to simulated-reader feedback just "writing to genre," the way a romance author plays to reader expectations? Turner doesn't resolve it as purely good or bad: "does that mean I'm like playing to the algorithm? Is that bad?... that's kind of just called writing to genre, right?" (Turner, 28:22.513)
6. **The opinion stake, stated as a claim:** AI didn't ghostwrite anything here — every word is still Turner's. What it replaced is the *infrastructure* of writing a novel: the sorting, the visibility into structure, the first pass of critique. The architecture became visible in a way it never was with sticky notes on a wall, and that's a different claim than "AI helps you write."

**Crafting direction:** This is the most personal and narrative-driven of the three, and the easiest one to accidentally flatten into "notes from our podcast" (checklist #11) — every beat must be rewritten as Turner's own essay about his own process, not a recap of a conversation about his process. Preserve the "compost heap" and "riff engine" metaphors exactly as spoken — both are strong, nameable, swyx-style candidate phrases that could recur across the series (per the brief's naming-compounds-distribution point). Expand beyond the tape with brief, concrete color on Larry Brooks's actual "nine sentences" framework (one or two sentences of outside explanation — this is the one spot in the post where a reader genuinely needs a definition to follow along) but do not expand into a general essay on story structure; keep the camera on Turner's specific build.
