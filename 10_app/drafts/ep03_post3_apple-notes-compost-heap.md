I have a hundred and fifty Apple Notes tagged #CH sitting on my phone, collected over something like a decade. CH is the old code name for the novel, *Counterfeit Heart*. Open any one of them and you get a fragment: an idea for a scene, a line a character might say, or just a note to myself about why something didn't work in the first draft. I don't organize them. I don't review them on any schedule. I treat them the way you'd treat a compost heap. Things go in, they sit, and eventually you come back and there's something usable underneath. "Treat the Apple Notes as like a compost heap for all my stories," is honestly just how I describe my own process to anyone who asks. <!-- Turner 23:49.065 -->

Before this year, turning that compost heap into an actual structured draft meant sitting down with all hundred and fifty notes open in some document, reading through them in order, grouping them by hand, and slowly rebuilding a sense of the story's shape from scratch every time I restarted the project. It worked. It also took forever, and a lot of what made it slow wasn't the writing, it was the sorting. "I throw spaghetti against the wall and this seems to be a nice trellis for me to throw it against to kind of figure a story out," is how I've described the whole approach for years. <!-- Turner 25:16.060 --> The trellis was always there. What I didn't have was a faster way to climb it.

Part of why the sorting mattered so much to me is that I need to see a book, physically, before I can tell if it works. "I really do need to see a kind of schematic," is how I described it. "I need lines. I need a spreadsheet. I need sort of a physical thing to kind of look at." <!-- Turner 22:42.376 --> Some authors get that from sticky notes covering a wall. I'd always gotten mine from a spreadsheet, built by hand, updated by hand, every single time I restarted a project.

This time, with an old first draft that wasn't quite landing and all hundred and fifty notes still sitting in my phone, I did something different. I fed the draft and the notes into Claude Code and asked it to do a first pass of sorting with me. Not for me. AI wasn't doing any actual writing here, so much as taking on the specific, mechanical, previously exhausting job of re-reading years of fragments and helping me figure out where each one belonged. The old version of this process was a real, hours-long sit at a desk, going through every note in order. This time, that same pass took a fraction of the time, which is the part that actually surprised me, since I expected the AI to help with the writing and instead it mostly helped with the thing before the writing.

**Building the tool instead of using a chat window**

Here's where it got genuinely new for me. Instead of working through a chat interface, I asked for something closer to an actual tool. "Let's make a riff engine," is what I landed on, "bring up one of the Apple Notes on the left and then just give me a box on the right to riff on it." <!-- Turner 26:4.765 --> One note at a time, visible, with space next to it to react, expand, argue with myself, or decide the thing didn't belong at all.

Three details about how that tool actually works matter more than the idea of it:

- It isn't a generic chat window. It's a purpose-built interface, split into exactly the two panes the task needs and nothing else, built around how I actually think through a note rather than around a general-purpose conversation format.
- It follows me, not the other way around. The same environment runs in the car, on the iPad, and on the phone, so the only thing standing between me and making progress is whether I feel like working right then, not whether I happen to be at a particular desk.
- It's built to feed a specific, named structural framework rather than produce vibes. Once a note has been riffed on enough to feel settled, the next stage pushes toward Larry Brooks's "nine sentences" method, a story-architecture framework I've used for about a decade, well before any of this. The riff engine has access to that framework and to my old first draft, so it isn't starting from nothing. It's starting from everything I'd already built, just finally visible in one place at once.

That last point is the one I'd underline. This didn't replace the framework I already trusted so much as hand it something it never had before: a direct, low-friction pipe from a decade of scattered raw material straight into the structure I already use to judge whether a story works.

**Letting the manuscript get read before I pay someone to read it**

The stage after structure is where this stopped being just a sorting tool and started being something I genuinely hadn't had access to before. Once I had a nine-sentence version of the story I trusted, I could take that same draft and run it past simulated beta readers and simulated critics, and get something back that reads like an actual workshop feedback report. "I can go ahead and send this out to simulated beta readers and simulated readers and simulated critics and start getting a workshop sort of feedback report back," is the plainest way I can describe it. <!-- Turner 27:55.070 --> I'd genuinely never had that option before. Feedback at that stage used to mean waiting for actual humans to be available, which is a real constraint on how fast you can iterate on something that isn't finished yet.

Jeremy's reaction connected this to something he'd already been doing on the sales side of his own work, simulating conversations with an ideal customer profile to pressure-test an offer before a real client ever saw it. "It provided insights that I had not thought of," he said, pointing out that a diversified set of simulated viewpoints tends to catch more than any single human reader would, "at a fraction of the cost." <!-- Jeremy 29:54.679 --> Two completely different domains landing on the same move independently: get a cheap, fast, rough version of outside judgment before you spend real money or real relationships on the expensive version.

And the expensive version is genuinely expensive. A developmental editor, the specific kind of editor who reads a full manuscript and gives you structural, story-level coaching rather than line edits, runs somewhere around two to three thousand dollars for a single pass. Simulated readers don't replace that pass, so much as clear the easy problems out before it starts, which means the money I do spend on a human editor goes toward the harder problems only a human would catch in the first place.

**The honest tension, because there is one**

I don't think this is uncomplicated, and I don't want to write it like it is. If the simulated feedback keeps flagging the same kinds of things, chapters where a reader's attention drifts, moments that don't land, there's a real question underneath that: am I just tuning the book to perform well against an algorithm's idea of what a reader wants? "Does that mean I'm like playing to the algorithm? Is that bad?" I asked myself out loud, mid-conversation. <!-- Turner 28:22.513 --> My own answer, for what it's worth, is that this is close to what writers have always called writing to genre. A romance author who studies what keeps readers turning pages and builds toward it isn't cheating. They're doing the job well. The simulated readers just make that feedback loop faster than it used to be, not fundamentally different in kind.

Where I'd actually draw a line is this: the tool can tell me a chapter doesn't land. It can't tell me what the right version of that chapter should feel like, because that's taste, and taste is the one input in this entire process that was never up for outsourcing. It's already caught several things I'd never have caught myself, mostly because I'm too close to my own book to see them, the same way any author is too close to their own pages. That's a real, useful function. It's a different thing entirely from someone else writing my book.

The honest answer, as of this writing, is that I don't know yet whether any of this actually makes the book better or just makes the process faster. The first simulated workshop report is sitting in front of me and I haven't finished deciding what I think of it. Is there signal in that noise, or did I just build an elaborate way to agree with myself faster? I'd rather say that out loud now than pretend I already know the answer.

**What actually changed, and what didn't**

Every word in *Counterfeit Heart* is still mine. I'm still writing every sentence, deciding what stays and what gets cut, deciding what the story is actually about underneath the plot. AI didn't write any of this. It cleared a path through the parts that weren't writing at all: sorting a decade of scattered notes, making the structure visible, and running a first pass of critique that used to mean months of waiting or thousands of dollars before I knew if a draft was even close.

A compost heap doesn't grow a plant for you. It just means the soil is finally ready when you're ready to plant something in it.
