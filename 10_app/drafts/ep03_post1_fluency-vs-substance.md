Someone dropped a twenty-seven-page report into a Monday meeting a couple of weeks ago. Hot off the presses, nobody had read it yet, including whoever had technically written it, in whatever sense "written" means these days. It had charts. It had structure. It read clean. By page three, people in the room were nodding along, me included for a minute, because it felt right, the particular way polished writing feels right before you've actually checked anything inside it.

Then I caught myself. I work in this industry. I'm supposed to be able to tell when something sounds smart and when it's just arranged to sound smart, and for a few pages in that meeting, I genuinely couldn't. "You could tell, like, wow, this really all sounds right," I said afterward, "but I can't tell if it's smart or totally unintelligible in some places." <!-- Turner 13:21.258 --> Fluency and substance had started costing the same two seconds to produce. A room full of professionals had just been handed the job of telling them apart, live, with no warning the job had changed.

To be fair to whoever wrote it, this wasn't pure filler. There were real graphs in there, real analysis, meat on the bone, and it would be a little disingenuous to pretend otherwise. That's actually what made it worse, not better. A report that's obviously garbage is easy to dismiss. A report that's seventy percent real and thirty percent confident-sounding air, mixed together at a sentence level, is the one that costs you something, because you have to read the whole thing closely just to find the thirty percent. "It's a risk, man," I said after. "It's a risk to get lost in that kind of loop." <!-- Turner 13:21.258 -->

I don't think the stakes on any single bad report are especially high right now. Everyone's experimenting, including whoever wrote this one, and that's a fine place for all of us to be collectively. But low stakes doesn't mean free. A slop bomb still soaks up a room's worth of time and attention before anyone catches it, and that time comes straight out of a budget none of us are getting more of.

**The job underneath the job**

Here's what I actually think is going on, and it's bigger than any one report. The more I hand off to agents, the more my own value creeps toward one specific activity: deciding. Reading what comes back and saying yes, no, or try again. "I actually think the best thing I can do for the productivity of the systems I run is like I sit at a desk and just read memos and be a decision engine for them, like, constantly," is how I put it to Jeremy. <!-- Turner 11:42.729 --> The funny part is how old that job actually is. Before the internet existed, people just called this being an executive. "It was people in suits sitting there reading memo after memo after memo, being a decision engine for team after team after team." <!-- Turner 12:11.422 -->

That reframe matters because it tells you which skill you're actually supposed to be building right now. It isn't prompting. I was coaching someone at work through a related problem this same week, and the analogy that fell out of my mouth was about a deck I'm building at home. It's not helpful to walk up to your contractor and say, hey, let me drive a few of these screws while you drive those, just to speed things along. It's also not helpful to stand there narrating how you think the stairs should've been framed. <!-- Turner 10:52.438 --> Neither move adds anything, and both waste the contractor's time. What you actually owe the job is ownership of what comes out the other end, especially the moment you're doing this somewhere professional with your name attached to the result.

That's the negotiation everyone's quietly running right now, whether they'd use these words or not: how much do I actually hand over to the agent. Not whether you can hand it over. How much, and under what conditions you still catch it if it's wrong.

**Why there's no shortcut around this**

Here's the piece I think gets skipped, and it's the one I most want to argue. You cannot be a decision engine, a filter, someone whose "I checked this" actually means something, unless you already know in detail what the finished output is supposed to look like. Try to delegate the thinking about what "good" looks like along with the task itself, and you haven't delegated anything. You've just added a step between yourself and a result you have no way to evaluate. There's no version of handing work off where you get to stay vague about the destination and still catch what's wrong on page nineteen. No free lunches.

Which means the skills that matter most right now aren't the ones most people are pricing in. Taste matters. A working sense of what the right shape of an answer looks like before you've seen it matters. Plain management ability, the kind that has nothing to do with which model you're running, matters most of all. Those used to be nice extras sitting on top of technical competence. They're load-bearing now, because they're the only thing standing between you and a twenty-seven-page report you nod through by accident.

So here's what's actually changed about how I work with agents since that meeting:

- I cap length before I ask for anything, as a forcing function rather than a style note. "Say, hey agent, max two pages, right? How hard is that?" <!-- Turner 13:52.681 --> A page limit alone won't make content better, but it does force the gap between fluent and substantive to show up faster, because there's nowhere left in two pages to hide it.
- I read everything before it leaves my hands, with no exceptions carved out for routine stuff. "I am not gonna ask anyone to read anything that I have not read word for word." <!-- Turner 14:21.992 --> I don't say that line to sound responsible. Saying it out loud, and actually meaning it, keeps me useful as a filter instead of a pass-through.
- I coach an agent's output the way I'd coach a person: once, deliberately, to calibrate what "done" looks like, and then I get out of the way. Going back to correct the same thing over and over isn't coaching anymore. It's a sign I skipped the step above.
- Before I delegate anything, I force myself to answer what the finished thing needs to look like, specifically, before I ask for it. If I can't answer that question, the real task isn't the one I was about to hand off.
- When I notice I'm vague about the destination, I treat getting specific as the real work, not an annoying prerequisite to it. Specificity first. Delegation second.

Jeremy landed on a version of the same rule independently, mid-conversation, about his own coding agents, from a completely different angle than mine: "I don't have time to read your twelve pages, buddy. Let me give me the fifty, fifty to a hundred words." <!-- Jeremy 12:45.611 --> Two of us, two different domains, arriving at the same number range without comparing notes beforehand. That's usually a decent sign an instinct is actually correct, not just a personal tic.

Jeremy had a comparison for the failure mode that I keep coming back to. Picture sand art: someone pours sand into a bucket, hands it to you, and asks what you think, except you've never actually looked inside. "You're just passing it off like, it looks kind of cool, I guess," is how he put it, "give me your authentic opinion on this." <!-- Jeremy 14:58.487 --> That's what forwarding an unread AI report actually is, dressed up as collaboration. He also pointed out the part I'd glossed over: even when you're reading closely, you're still only the filter, not the author of the judgment underneath it. "Yeah, but you're at least the filter to help them out," he said. <!-- Jeremy 14:52.237 --> At least is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Being the filter isn't a consolation prize for not being able to grab the tools yourself. Right now, it's close to the entire job.

**Where this leaves the job**

I keep coming back to the idea that the executive job isn't dissolving into AI. It's reverting to something older. The heavy personal involvement in drafting and building and formatting that made the job feel modern for the last couple of decades was mostly a side effect of the internet making everyone their own analyst. That side effect is unwinding fast. What's underneath looks a lot like memo after memo after memo, which was the job all along, just without the busywork we'd all mistaken for the job itself.

The new skill isn't prompting, and it isn't knowing which model to run this quarter. It's deciding, deliberately and ahead of time, how many pages you're willing to accept before you lose the ability to tell smart from unintelligible. Everything else, the instinct for taste, for style, for plain management, is just what fills in the space once you've drawn that line honestly instead of hoping you won't need it.

This is the first of two posts Jeremy and I are writing about what managing agents actually looks like day to day, as opposed to what the thinkpieces predict it looks like. The next one picks up exactly where "no free lunches" gets uncomfortable: what happens when an agent makes a decision on your behalf that you never got a chance to accept or reject in the first place.
