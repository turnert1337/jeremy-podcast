2 · Tags — the research, verbatim recommendation, and why only two

What tags actually are on Substack: not hashtags, not search keywords. Each tag auto-generates an archive page collecting your posts under that label — they're shelving for YOUR readers, not broadcast. Discovery is driven by the publication category (leaderboards, browse) and, for the podcast, by the category dropdowns in item 1. There's live debate about whether tags feed the app's suggested-posts engine (someone ran a real experiment; Substack's own position downplays it) — treat any discovery effect as a bonus.

How people actually use them: a handful of stable, reader-facing labels that map to the publication's content types — so "browse all episodes" or "browse all essays" is one click. The failure mode is hashtag-thinking: ten one-off tags = ten orphaned archive pages.

Verbatim, on every episode post:

Podcast   AI

Why these two: Podcast becomes your de-facto episodes archive (matters from episode 2 onward, and separates the show from future Lossy essays). AI is Substack's own topic vocabulary — the platform curates a topic page literally at substack.com/topics/ai, so if tags ever do feed suggestions, you're aligned with their naming. Why not more: each tag is an archive page you're implicitly promising to maintain; "ai-agents" was my earlier invention, not an established convention (you were right to call it); and the discovery lever tags don't pull is already pulled by your categories. Future Lossy essays get Essay — that's the whole taxonomy.

Sources: Substack Writers at Work (tags/groups/sections explained) · reallygoodbusinessideas.com (categories vs tags) · pubstacksuccess (tagging discoverability experiment) · substack.com/topics/ai
